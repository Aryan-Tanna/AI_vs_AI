First compare each side's arguments with what the real court held, and look for these mistakes in how it argued (judge the quality of the argument, not who won):
- MISSED_EVIDENCE: a record item the court relied on that the side never used, or used weakly.
- UNANSWERED_POINT: an opposing point the court found decisive that the side never answered (the verifier's map shows which points went unanswered).
- WRONG_LAW_VERSION: the side argued a provision, threshold or period that was not the law on the case's dates (for example a later amendment).
- MISSTATED_LAW: the side stated a provision, its conditions or a precedent's rule wrongly.
- WEAK_AUTHORITY: the side relied on an authority that did not support its point, or missed one the court used.
- MISREAD_FACT: the side treated a contested fact as settled, or read the record wrongly.
- STRATEGY: the side led with a weak point, conceded too early, or framed its case on the wrong issue.
- OPPONENT_TECHNIQUE: a method the OTHER side used to good effect (a way of framing an issue, of linking record items, of using or distinguishing an authority, of sequencing a submission) that this side could use for ITS OWN position in a similar situation. Learn the method, never the other side's position; the lesson is for the side that did not use it.
- OTHER: anything else general and useful.
Look at both sides for each lesson: what this side did, and what the other side did that worked against it (the verifier's map shows which points landed and which went unanswered).
Only a mistake the side could have avoided with what it had: the record, the statutes and the case library it was given. If the court decided on evidence the agents never had, write no lesson on it.

Every lesson is about METHOD, never about what the law is.
- A lesson never states what a provision means, what a court held, which reading is correct, which date or event is decisive, or that the law favours one kind of party. Those answers differ between cases and periods, and a lesson that carries them would push later hearings toward one view of the law.
- The record is closed. Advocates cannot produce, request or inspect documents, call evidence, or add facts; the bench cannot either. Their only moves are: citing record items, looking up provisions, precedents and their own lessons, arguing inferences labelled as inferences, conceding, and answering the other side. A lesson recommends only these moves, never "request the document" or "produce the record".
- A lesson says what to DO in a kind of situation: which question to settle first, what to look up (a provision, a precedent, the record), which record items to marshal, which attack to expect, how to answer it, how to structure or sequence the submission, what to avoid asserting without support.
- A lesson refers to the law only by its role ("the limitation provision", "the provision under which the application is made", "the authorities in your library", "a threshold introduced by amendment"). It never names a section, regulation, precedent or case, and never contains a number, amount, period or date: those are looked up for each case and its dates, never carried in memory.
- Decisive provisions (a bar, a threshold, a time limit, an ineligibility) are worth a lesson about checking them first, never about their content.
Wrong: "A claim admitted by the resolution professional is a verified claim." (a legal conclusion)
Right: "When a plan treats verified and unverified claims differently, look up the plan clause and the provisions on verification, and show from the record when and how the claim was admitted before relying on either category."
Wrong: "The minimum default is one crore rupees." / Right: "Before the merits, look up the bars, thresholds and time limits of the provision invoked, as they stood on the case's dates, and test them against the record's amounts and dates; a bar or threshold can decide the case on its own."
Wrong: "Pending civil suits do not bar admission." / Right: "When the other side relies on a pending suit, address whether the provision under which the application is made makes a parallel dispute relevant, with a looked-up authority, before turning to the facts."

Types:
- "ADVOCACY" (for_whom "PETITIONER" or "RESPONDENT"): a strategy that side could have used to argue ITS OWN position better: a decisive point it missed or made weakly, research it should have done, record items it should have used, an attack it should have expected and how to meet it. Never tell a side to abandon, concede or avoid its own position.
- "LEGAL_RULE" (for_whom "BENCH"): a decision method the simulated bench missed: what it should have examined, in which order, and against which record items and sources, before deciding an issue of this kind. State the method, never the answer.
- "PROCEDURAL_ERROR" (for_whom the side): only for a verifier error code listed above for that side; "error_code" is that code; the lesson says how to avoid it.

Rules for every lesson:
- General: it must help in a different case with different parties. Describe the situation in "trigger" in general terms.
- No names of any party, person, company, bank, court or pseudonym, no case numbers, no dates of this case, and no "in this case".
- A method, a tactic or an error to avoid, never a statistic or tendency ("creditors usually win" is forbidden) and never a statement of the law.
- "issue_ids" and "source_paras" are copied from the court's findings above that the lesson rests on.
- "statute_ids" are the statute IDs above whose situations the lesson concerns.
